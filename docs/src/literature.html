Part 2: Detailed summaries

A. Density-based clustering foundations

[1] Ester, M., Kriegel, H.-P., Sander, J., & Xu, X. (1996). A density-based algorithm for discovering clusters in large spatial databases with noise. Proc. KDD-96, 226–231. AAAI Press.
✅ Verified (AAAI proceedings PDF retrieved; no DOI)standard account

What it does. Introduces DBSCAN. The paper defines clusters as maximal sets of density-connected points, based on two parameters (Eps and MinPts), and defines core points, border points and noise formally. It proves that clusters are uniquely determined (up to border-point ambiguity) and gives an algorithm that uses a spatial index (R*-tree) for region queries.

Key ideas. Clusters of arbitrary shape; no need to specify the number of clusters; explicit noise. For parameter choice it proposes the sorted k-dist graph: plot every point's distance to its k-th nearest neighbour in descending order and set Eps at the first "valley" (the knee). It suggests k = 4 (MinPts = 4) for 2-D data, since larger k gave similar results at higher cost. Experiments on synthetic data and the SEQUOIA 2000 benchmark show DBSCAN finding non-convex clusters that CLARANS misses, at much lower run-time.

Relevance to UA-DBSCAN. This is the base algorithm. Its k-dist heuristic is the "ad hoc" ε-selection your definition aims to replace, so it is the primary baseline for part (ii).

[2] Sander, J., Ester, M., Kriegel, H.-P., & Xu, X. (1998). Density-based clustering in spatial databases: The algorithm GDBSCAN and its applications. Data Mining and Knowledge Discovery, 2(2), 169–194. doi:10.1023/A:1009745219419
✅ Verifiedstandard account

What it does. Generalizes DBSCAN in two ways. The ε-neighbourhood becomes any neighbourhood predicate that is symmetric and reflexive, and the "at least MinPts" condition becomes any minimum weighted cardinality. With this, the algorithm can cluster polygons and other extended objects, and can use non-spatial attributes. Applications include astronomy, biology, earth science and geography.

Key ideas. The correctness of density-based clustering needs only these abstract properties, not Euclidean geometry. It also contains the MinPts = 2·dim heuristic, cited by Schubert et al. [5].

Relevance. This is the theoretical justification for part (i). A neighbourhood defined by an uncertainty-scaled space–time distance is a valid GDBSCAN predicate, so DBSCAN's cluster properties still hold. Cite it when you argue that your non-Euclidean, non-metric distance is legitimate.

[3] Ankerst, M., Breunig, M. M., Kriegel, H.-P., & Sander, J. (1999). OPTICS: Ordering points to identify the clustering structure. ACM SIGMOD Record, 28(2), 49–60. doi:10.1145/304181.304187
✅ Verifiedstandard account

What it does. Instead of producing a single clustering for one ε, OPTICS computes an ordering of the points plus each point's core-distance and reachability-distance. Plotting reachability in this order gives a reachability plot: valleys are clusters, and nested valleys are hierarchical sub-clusters.

Key ideas. One run encodes the density-based clusterings for a whole range of ε. Any DBSCAN result (for ε below the generating distance) can be extracted by a horizontal cut. Automatic extraction of hierarchical clusters (the "ξ" method) is also proposed.

Relevance. This is the classical multi-scale density method. Your cluster-count curve over ε is a compressed summary of the same information. Use OPTICS as a baseline, and as a visual diagnostic of multi-scale damage structure.

[4] Campello, R. J. G. B., Moulavi, D., & Sander, J. (2013). Density-based clustering based on hierarchical density estimates. In PAKDD 2013, LNCS 7819, 160–172. doi:10.1007/978-3-642-37456-2_14
✅ Verifiedstandard account

What it does. Introduces HDBSCAN. It builds the complete hierarchy of DBSCAN*-style clusterings over all density thresholds (via a minimum spanning tree on the mutual reachability distance), condenses it using a minimum cluster size, and extracts the flat clustering that maximizes total cluster stability, which measures how long clusters persist across density levels.

Key ideas. It removes the need for a global ε, handles clusters of varying density, and still labels noise. The main parameter is the minimum cluster size.

Relevance. This is the strongest modern baseline and a conceptual sibling of part (ii): both select clusters by persistence across density scales. To be publishable, UA-DBSCAN must show what it adds over HDBSCAN: a physically interpretable ε, an uncertainty-aware distance, and a noise class with physical meaning. HDBSCAN can also be run with your UA distance, which is a useful ablation.

[5] Schubert, E., Sander, J., Ester, M., Kriegel, H.-P., & Xu, X. (2017). DBSCAN revisited, revisited: Why and how you should (still) use DBSCAN. ACM Transactions on Database Systems, 42(3), Art. 19, 1–21. doi:10.1145/3068335
✅ Verifiedfull text §3.3–4.4 read

What it does. A response by the original authors to a SIGMOD 2015 paper that had criticized DBSCAN's run-time. It clarifies the algorithm's complexity and the role of index structures, and re-runs benchmarks. Most usefully for you, it gives practical parameter recommendations (§4.1–4.2).

Key ideas. (1) MinPts can often stay at 4 for 2-D data, or 2·dim following [2]; increase it for noisy, large or duplicate-heavy data. (2) ε "should be chosen as small as possible", ideally from domain knowledge, otherwise from the k-distance plot. (3) Red flags for degenerate results: the desirable amount of noise is usually between 1% and 30%, and if the largest component holds more than 20–50% of clustered points, ε is too large or a hierarchical method (OPTICS or HDBSCAN) should be used. (4) Figure 3 of the paper plots the share of clustered points, the size of the largest component, and the number of clusters as a function of ε, and uses these curves to find the useful range of ε. (5) DBSCAN can be paired with any distance function, which grid-based accelerations cannot do.

Relevance. This is the most important precedent for part (ii). Your noise-fraction constraint and cluster-count curve extend their informal heuristics into a formal selection rule. Cite it prominently, and position your contribution as making these heuristics rigorous and physically grounded.

[6] Birant, D., & Kut, A. (2007). ST-DBSCAN: An algorithm for clustering spatial–temporal data. Data & Knowledge Engineering, 60(1), 208–221. doi:10.1016/j.datak.2006.01.013
✅ Verifiedstandard account

What it does. Extends DBSCAN to spatio-temporal data with two distance parameters, Eps1 for spatial distance and Eps2 for non-spatial (e.g. temporal or attribute) distance. A point's neighbours must satisfy both. It adds a density factor to reduce problems with adjacent clusters of different density, and compares values against cluster averages to avoid chaining through slowly drifting attributes. Demonstrated on sea-surface temperature and similar geo-data.

Relevance. This is the most direct prior art for combining location and time (part i). Your approach differs by (a) using one combined, dimensionless distance and (b) scaling space by per-event uncertainty. ST-DBSCAN is an essential baseline, and "two thresholds versus one scaled distance" is a design choice you should discuss.

[7] Kriegel, H.-P., & Pfeifle, M. (2005). Density-based clustering of uncertain data. Proc. 11th ACM SIGKDD, 672–677. doi:10.1145/1081870.1081955
✅ Verifiedstandard account

What it does. Proposes FDBSCAN (fuzzy DBSCAN) for objects whose positions are known only as probability distributions. Distances between uncertain objects become distance distribution functions, giving the probability that two objects lie within ε of each other. Core-object and reachability properties become probabilistic, and objects are clustered accordingly.

Relevance. This is the closest data-mining ancestor of part (i). UA-DBSCAN differs in that the uncertainty is physically derived (sensor geometry, wave speed, onset picking), the distance is a closed-form Mahalanobis-type expression, and time is included. You should also consider the FDBSCAN-style probabilistic predicate as an alternative distance.

B. Parameter selection and cluster validation

[8] Rahmah, N., & Sitanggang, I. S. (2016). Determination of optimal epsilon (Eps) value on DBSCAN algorithm to clustering data on peatland hotspots in Sumatra. IOP Conf. Ser.: Earth Environ. Sci., 31, 012012. doi:10.1088/1755-1315/31/1/012012
✅ Verifiedstandard account

What it does. An applied study that chooses ε for clustering geographic hotspot points by locating the knee of the sorted k-distance curve, then clusters the data with DBSCAN.

Relevance. It is a representative example of the k-distance-knee practice widely used in applied papers. It is useful for showing that ε selection in practice is still largely visual, which motivates your automated, physically grounded rule. It is a minor paper, so cite it as an example rather than as an authority.

[9] Starczewski, A., Goetzen, P., & Er, M. J. (2020). A new method for automatic determining of the DBSCAN parameters. J. Artificial Intelligence and Soft Computing Research, 10(3), 209–221. doi:10.2478/jaiscr-2020-0014
✅ Verifiedstandard account

What it does. Proposes an automatic procedure for estimating ε (and the associated MinPts) from the statistics of nearest-neighbour distances, removing the manual knee reading, and tests it on synthetic and benchmark datasets.

Relevance. It represents the "automatic ε" literature. None of these methods use measurement uncertainty. That is the gap your physics-based ε fills. Consider it as a secondary baseline for ε selection.

[10] von Luxburg, U. (2010). Clustering stability: An overview. Foundations and Trends in Machine Learning, 2(3), 235–274. doi:10.1561/2200000008
✅ Verifiedstandard account

What it does. Surveys the principle of choosing clustering parameters (usually the number of clusters) by stability: good parameters give clusterings that change little under resampling or perturbation of the data. It discusses when this works, the theory behind it (including known failure cases), and practical protocols.

Relevance. This is the theoretical backbone for the "plateau = stable = real structure" argument in part (ii). It also warns that stability is not the same as correctness, which you should acknowledge. It suggests a strong extension: perturb event locations according to Σ (bootstrap) and measure how stable the clusters are.

[11] Moulavi, D., Jaskowiak, P. A., Campello, R. J. G. B., Zimek, A., & Sander, J. (2014). Density-based clustering validation. Proc. 2014 SIAM Int. Conf. on Data Mining, 839–847. doi:10.1137/1.9781611973440.96
✅ Verifiedstandard account

What it does. Introduces DBCV, an internal validity index for density-based clusterings. It compares the lowest-density region inside each cluster with the highest-density region between clusters, and so handles arbitrary shapes and noise, where the silhouette and similar indices fail.

Relevance. Use it as the internal metric for evaluating UA-DBSCAN on real data without ground truth. You could also compare "plateau-selected ε" with "DBCV-maximizing ε".

[12] Rousseeuw, P. J. (1987). Silhouettes: A graphical aid to the interpretation and validation of cluster analysis. J. Computational and Applied Mathematics, 20, 53–65. doi:10.1016/0377-0427(87)90125-7
✅ Verifiedstandard account

What it does. Defines the silhouette value of each object: (b − a)/max(a, b), where a is the mean distance to the object's own cluster and b is the mean distance to the nearest other cluster. The average silhouette is used to judge and choose clusterings.

Relevance. This is the standard internal metric that reviewers expect. It is biased towards compact, convex clusters, so report it alongside DBCV and explain its limits for crack-shaped clusters.

[13] Hubert, L., & Arabie, P. (1985). Comparing partitions. Journal of Classification, 2(1), 193–218. doi:10.1007/BF01908075
✅ Verifiedstandard account

What it does. Derives the Adjusted Rand Index (ARI), a chance-corrected measure of agreement between two partitions. It is 1 for identical partitions and has an expected value of 0 for random ones.

Relevance. ARI is the main external metric on synthetic data with known clusters. It can also be used to define a stronger plateau criterion, where neighbouring ε values give nearly identical partitions, rather than merely equal cluster counts.

C. Acoustic emission fundamentals and damage indicators

[14] Grosse, C. U., & Ohtsu, M. (Eds.) (2008). Acoustic Emission Testing. Springer. doi:10.1007/978-3-540-69972-9
✅ Verifiedstandard account

What it is. An edited textbook covering AE fundamentals: sources and wave propagation, sensors and instrumentation, signal-based versus parameter-based analysis, source localization, moment tensor analysis (Ohtsu's chapter on SiGMA), and applications in concrete, metals, composites and geotechnics. A second edition appeared in 2022 (Springer Tracts in Civil Engineering).

Relevance. This is your main textbook for the AE side. Read the chapters on localization and on signal-based analysis first.

[15] Ono, K. (2018). Review on structural health evaluation with acoustic emission. Applied Sciences, 8(6), 958. doi:10.3390/app8060958
✅ Verifiedstandard account (open access)

What it does. A broad, open-access review of AE for structural health evaluation by a senior figure in the field. It covers source mechanisms, instrumentation, signal features, source location, and applications to pressure vessels, bridges, concrete, composites and more, including practical assessment criteria.

Relevance. This is the best free starting point for learning how AE is used in practice, and a good source for the introduction section of your paper.

[16] Lockner, D. (1993). The role of acoustic emission in the study of rock fracture. Int. J. Rock Mech. Min. Sci. & Geomech. Abstr., 30(7), 883–899. doi:10.1016/0148-9062(93)90041-B
✅ Verifiedstandard account

What it does. Reviews AE in laboratory rock fracture. It shows that located AE events track micro-crack growth, their clustering and the nucleation of a macroscopic fault, and discusses AE statistics (e.g. b-value) as analogues of earthquake behaviour.

Relevance. This is the classic reference for spatio-temporal AE clouds localizing into a fracture: exactly the structure UA-DBSCAN tries to extract. It also bridges AE to seismological clustering methods (Part F).

[17] Ohtsu, M. (2010). Recommendation of RILEM TC 212-ACD: Acoustic emission and related NDE techniques for crack detection and damage evaluation in concrete. Test method for classification of active cracks in concrete structures by acoustic emission. Materials and Structures, 43(9), 1187–1189. doi:10.1617/s11527-010-9640-6
✅ Verifiedstandard account

What it is. A short RILEM technical-committee recommendation that standardizes how active cracks in concrete are classified from AE parameters, principally by plotting the RA value against the average frequency to separate tensile-type from shear-type cracking.

Relevance. This is the accepted feature-based criterion for validating your clusters and noise class. For example, check whether damage clusters show the RA–AF evolution expected for developing cracks while the "benign" noise class does not.

[18] Ohno, K., & Ohtsu, M. (2010). Crack classification in concrete based on acoustic emission. Construction and Building Materials, 24(12), 2339–2346. doi:10.1016/j.conbuildmat.2010.05.004
✅ Verifiedstandard account

What it does. Compares parameter-based crack classification (RA versus average frequency) with moment tensor analysis (SiGMA) on concrete specimens, examining how well the simple parameter-based criterion agrees with the physically based moment tensor classification.

Relevance. It shows the strengths and limits of RA–AF classification, which you will use to interpret cluster and noise events.

[19] Aggelis, D. G. (2011). Classification of cracking mode in concrete by acoustic emission parameters. Mechanics Research Communications, 38(3), 153–157. doi:10.1016/j.mechrescom.2011.03.007
✅ Verifiedstandard account

What it does. Uses bending and shear-type tests on concrete to show that the tensile and shear phases of fracture produce different AE waveform parameters (RA and average frequency), supporting RA–AF-based classification of the cracking mode.

Relevance. Together with [17] and [18], this gives experimental backing for using waveform features to assign physical meaning to clusters.

[20] Colombo, S., Main, I. G., & Forde, M. C. (2003). Assessing damage of reinforced concrete beam using "b-value" analysis of acoustic emission signals. J. Materials in Civil Engineering, 15(3), 280–286. doi:10.1061/(ASCE)0899-1561(2003)15:3(280)
✅ Verifiedstandard account

What it does. Applies the seismological b-value (slope of the amplitude–frequency distribution) to AE from reinforced concrete beams under load, relating changes in b-value to the progression from micro-cracking to macro-cracking.

Relevance. This gives a per-cluster damage indicator: compute the b-value inside each UA-DBSCAN cluster and inside the noise class. A decreasing b-value in clusters but not in noise would support the "benign" interpretation in part (iii).

D. Source localization and its uncertainty

[21] Grosse, C. U., Reinhardt, H. W., & Dahm, T. (1997). Localization and classification of fracture types in concrete with quantitative acoustic emission measurement techniques. NDT & E International, 30(4), 223–230. doi:10.1016/S0963-8695(96)00060-6
✅ Verifiedstandard account

What it does. Describes quantitative AE in concrete: 3-D source localization from arrival times with multi-sensor arrays (seismology-style algorithms), and fracture-type classification from waveform analysis (moment tensor inversion).

Relevance. It shows the standard location pipeline that produces the events you will cluster, and makes clear that location precision in concrete is limited by heterogeneity and onset picking.

[22] Kurz, J. H., Grosse, C. U., & Reinhardt, H.-W. (2005). Strategies for reliable automatic onset time picking of acoustic emissions and of ultrasound signals in concrete. Ultrasonics, 43(7), 538–546. doi:10.1016/j.ultras.2004.12.005
✅ Verifiedstandard account

What it does. Compares automatic onset pickers for AE and ultrasound in concrete, notably the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) picker, against manual picking. It proposes strategies that make picking more reliable and assesses the resulting accuracy.

Relevance. Onset picking error (σt) is the input to your covariance model Σ = σt²(JTJ)−1. This paper helps you choose and justify realistic σt values.

[23] Kundu, T. (2014). Acoustic source localization. Ultrasonics, 54(1), 25–38. doi:10.1016/j.ultras.2013.06.009
✅ Verifiedstandard account

What it does. A review of acoustic source localization techniques for isotropic and anisotropic plates and structures: triangulation with known wave speed, optimization-based methods, methods for unknown or direction-dependent wave speed, sensor-cluster techniques, and beamforming, with their assumptions and limitations.

Relevance. This is the best single entry point to localization. It explains why localization accuracy depends on material and sensor configuration, which is the physical premise of part (i).

[24] Hensman, J., Mills, R., Pierce, S. G., Worden, K., & Eaton, M. (2010). Locating acoustic emission sources in complex structures using Gaussian processes. Mechanical Systems and Signal Processing, 24(1), 211–223. doi:10.1016/j.ymssp.2009.05.018
✅ Verifiedstandard account

What it does. Instead of assuming a wave speed and straight paths, it learns the mapping between source position and arrival-time differences from training sources (e.g. pencil-lead breaks) using Gaussian-process regression. This works on geometrically complex structures and gives a probabilistic estimate of location.

Relevance. This is a data-driven route to per-event uncertainty. The GP predictive variance can feed UA-DBSCAN directly, which matters for complex geometries where Equation (3.3) of the guide is inaccurate.

[25] Schumacher, T., Straub, D., & Higgins, C. (2012). Toward a probabilistic acoustic emission source location algorithm: A Bayesian approach. Journal of Sound and Vibration, 331(19), 4233–4245. doi:10.1016/j.jsv.2012.04.028
✅ Verifiedstandard account

What it does. Formulates AE source location as Bayesian inference. Arrival-time errors and the uncertain wave speed are modelled probabilistically, and Markov chain Monte Carlo sampling yields a posterior probability distribution of the source location instead of a point estimate. It is demonstrated on concrete specimens.

Relevance. This is the key paper for obtaining per-event uncertainty. Its posterior is exactly the object UA-DBSCAN's distance should use. You can summarize each posterior by its mean and covariance, or use the full samples with an FDBSCAN-style [7] probabilistic distance.

[26] Madarshahian, R., Ziehl, P., & Caicedo, J. M. (2019). Acoustic emission Bayesian source location: Onset time challenge. Mechanical Systems and Signal Processing, 123, 483–495. doi:10.1016/j.ymssp.2019.01.021
✅ Verifiedstandard account

What it does. Extends Bayesian AE location by treating the onset-time picking error explicitly within the probabilistic model, since mis-picked onsets are a dominant source of location error, and shows improved location and uncertainty estimates.

Relevance. It gives more realistic Σ estimates and supports the argument that uncertainty is event-specific (it depends on each event's signal quality, not only on geometry).

[27] Yin, S., Xiao, H., Xu, C., Deng, M., & Kundu, T. (2023). Theoretical error formation and evaluation of acoustic source localization for cluster-based techniques. Ultrasonics, 132, 106982. doi:10.1016/j.ultras.2023.106982
⚠️ metadata only

What it does (from title and venue). A theoretical analysis of how localization errors form, and how to evaluate them, for "cluster-based" localization techniques. Here "cluster" means small groups of closely spaced sensors, not event clustering.

Relevance. It is likely useful for building an analytical model of how sensor geometry shapes Σ. Read the paper before citing it for specifics.

E. Clustering and machine learning in AE

[28] Godin, N., Huguet, S., Gaertner, R., & Salmon, L. (2004). Clustering of acoustic emission signals collected during tensile tests on unidirectional glass/polyester composite using supervised and unsupervised classifiers. NDT & E International, 37(4), 253–264. doi:10.1016/j.ndteint.2003.09.010
✅ Verifiedstandard account

What it does. Clusters AE signals from composite tensile tests in waveform-feature space, using unsupervised k-means and a Kohonen self-organizing map and then a supervised classifier, to separate damage mechanisms such as matrix cracking, fibre–matrix debonding and fibre breakage.

Relevance. This is a classic of AE clustering. It shows the dominant paradigm (clustering signals by mechanism) that your work complements with clustering events by location and time.

[29] Sause, M. G. R., Gribov, A., Unwin, A. R., & Horn, S. (2012). Pattern recognition approach to identify natural clusters of acoustic emission signals. Pattern Recognition Letters, 33(1), 17–23. doi:10.1016/j.patrec.2011.09.018
✅ Verifiedstandard account

What it does. Addresses the key weakness of k-means-type AE clustering, namely choosing the number of clusters and the features. It proposes an exhaustive procedure over feature combinations and cluster numbers, scored with several validity indices, to find the "natural" clusters in AE data from composites.

Relevance. It shows that parameter selection is a recognized, unsolved problem in AE clustering, which directly supports the motivation for part (ii). Its methodology (combining several validity criteria) is a model for your evaluation.

[30] Farhidzadeh, A., Salamone, S., & Singla, P. (2013). A probabilistic approach for damage identification and crack mode classification in reinforced concrete structures. J. Intelligent Material Systems and Structures, 24(14), 1722–1735. doi:10.1177/1045389X13484101
✅ Verifiedstandard account

What it does. Uses Gaussian mixture modelling of AE parameters (RA and average frequency) from reinforced concrete tests to classify crack modes probabilistically, giving each event a probability of being tensile or shear rather than a hard label.

Relevance. It is a good example of probabilistic interpretation of AE and a way to attach soft mechanism labels to your spatial clusters. It is also a model-based alternative to density-based clustering that you can contrast in your literature review.

[31] Ramasso, E., Mbarga Nkogo, M., Chandarana, N., Bourbon, G., Le Moal, P., Lefèbvre, Q., Personeni, M., Soutis, C., Gresil, M., & Thibaud, S. (2025). What it does (from title; an arXiv preprint version is titled "A Novel Criterion for Interpreting Acoustic Emission Damage Signals Based on Cluster Onset Distribution"). Proposes a criterion that uses the timing of when clusters first appear (cluster onsets) during a test to interpret and select AE clusterings, and demonstrates it on three monitoring case studies.

Relevance. It is recent work that brings time into AE cluster interpretation, and relates to both the temporal term in part (i) and the question of choosing among clusterings. Read the full text before citing it.

It explores how ML enables the study of more complex phenomena and discusses the conditions, precautions and limitations of its use, and future prospects.

Relevance. This is the most up-to-date survey for your related-work section. Use it to show where density-based, location-space clustering sits (or is missing) in current AE-ML practice.

F. Seismology and microseismic analogues

[33] Zaliapin, I., & Ben-Zion, Y. (2013). Earthquake clusters in southern California I: Identification and stability. J. Geophysical Research: Solid Earth, 118(6), 2847–2864. doi:10.1002/jgrb.50179
✅ Verifiedstandard account

What it does. Identifies earthquake clusters using a nearest-neighbour proximity that combines inter-event time, spatial distance (scaled by fractal dimension) and magnitude. The distribution of this proximity is bimodal, separating clustered from background seismicity. The authors test the stability of the identified clusters with respect to catalogue uncertainties and parameter choices.

Relevance. This is a well-established space–time distance for event catalogues, and a principled way to separate clustered from background events, analogous to your noise class. Its stability analysis parallels your plateau idea. It is a strong alternative distance to compare against.

[34] Wang, Y., Ouillon, G., Woessner, J., Sornette, D., & Husen, S. (2013). Automatic reconstruction of fault networks from seismicity catalogs including location uncertainty. J. Geophysical Research: Solid Earth, 118(11), 5956–5975. doi:10.1002/2013JB010164
✅ Verifiedabstract read

What it does. Introduces ACLUD (anisotropic clustering of location uncertainty distributions), which reconstructs active fault networks from earthquake locations and their individual location uncertainties. Among many candidate fault networks, the best is chosen with six validation procedures (cross-validation, BIC, and four based on agreement with focal mechanisms). Tests on synthetic catalogues show good recovery, especially with the BIC and focal-mechanism criteria, even with substantial background seismicity. Applied to the Landers area, the results stress the importance of spatially inhomogeneous location uncertainties.

Relevance. This is the closest prior art in spirit: clustering events while explicitly using per-event location uncertainty. It differs in fitting planar fault segments rather than density clusters. Reviewers from geophysics will know it, so you must cite it and explain how UA-DBSCAN differs (density-based, no planar-shape assumption, explicit noise class, AE scale).

[35] Matochkina, S. D., Shebalin, P. N., Smirnov, V. B., Ponomarev, A. V., & Malyutin, P. A. (2024). Acoustic emission events clustering parameters in laboratory rock fracture experiments. Fizika Zemli, (5), 85–96. doi:10.31857/S0002333724050066
✅ Verifiedabstract read

What it does. Tests whether the earthquake productivity law holds for AE in laboratory rock fracture, using AE catalogues from uniaxial loading of Westerly granite and Bentheim sandstone (USGS Menlo Park and GFZ Potsdam data). It finds a unimodal distribution of the nearest-neighbour proximity function for these AE events (unlike the bimodal one in tectonic seismicity), and that the productivity law holds.

Relevance. This is direct evidence that seismological space–time clustering tools transfer to AE, with an important caveat: the clean clustered/background separation seen in earthquakes may not appear in lab AE. This is a useful warning for part (iii). (The journal is Russian-language; an English translation appears in Izvestiya, Physics of the Solid Earth.)

[36] Anikiev, D., Birnie, C., bin Waheed, U., Alkhalifah, T., Gu, C., Verschuur, D. J., & Eisner, L. (2023). Machine learning in microseismic monitoring. Earth-Science Reviews, 239, 104371. doi:10.1016/j.earscirev.2023.104371
✅ Verifiedstandard account

What it does. A comprehensive review of machine learning across the microseismic workflow: event detection, arrival picking, location, source characterization, and interpretation, including clustering of event clouds.

Relevance. Microseismic monitoring is AE at field scale. This review shows which ML ideas, including clustering of located events, have been tried at larger scales and could transfer to AE.

[37] Vijay, R. K., Nanda, S. J., & Sharma, A. (2025). A review on clustering algorithms for spatiotemporal seismicity analysis. Artificial Intelligence Review, 58(8), 231. doi:10.1007/s10462-025-11229-3
✅ Verifiedabstract read

What it does. Reviews data-driven earthquake clustering algorithms, models and mechanisms for seismological applications, including a short review of the empirical laws of statistical seismology and of declustering (separating triggered from background events).

Relevance. This is a map of spatio-temporal event clustering methods (including DBSCAN variants) from a neighbouring field. Declustering is the seismological twin of your "noise as benign background" idea.

[38] Sun, X., Lu, R., Wang, W., Yang, F., & He, K. (2025). Automated 3-D modelling of seismic faults using adaptive threshold hierarchical clustering and quantitative assessment. Geophysical Journal International, 244(1), ggaf426. doi:10.1093/gji/ggaf426
✅ Verifiedabstract read

What it does. Builds 3-D fault models automatically from relocated hypocentres. It uses a nearest-neighbour index to test whether seismicity is clustered, adaptive-threshold hierarchical clustering to find the event clusters belonging to each fault, automatic slicing and surface interpolation to model the faults, and kernel density estimates of event-to-fault distances to assess and optimize the models. The motivation is that manual interpretation is non-unique because of uneven hypocentre distributions and location uncertainty.

Relevance. This is a recent example of adaptive (data-driven) threshold selection for clustering located events, conceptually parallel to your ε rule. It is a good citation for the claim that fixed thresholds are a recognized problem.

Part 3: Synthesis — what the literature says and where the gap is

3.1 What is well established

  • Density-based clustering is well understood. DBSCAN [1] and its generalization [2] define clusters by density-connectivity and label noise. Multi-scale variants [3, 4] avoid a single ε. Heuristics for ε exist [1, 5, 8, 9], but they are geometric (k-distance knees) or informal (noise 1–30%, largest cluster below 20–50% [5]).
  • Spatio-temporal [6] and uncertain-data [7] extensions of DBSCAN exist in data mining, but separately and without a physical model of the uncertainty.
  • AE source localization is inherently uncertain, and the uncertainty depends on onset picking [22], wave-speed assumptions and material [21, 23], and sensor geometry [27]. Bayesian and GP methods can quantify it per event [24–26].
  • AE clustering is common, but overwhelmingly in waveform-feature space to separate damage mechanisms [28–30, 32], and choosing the number of clusters is a known difficulty [29].
  • Seismology has mature tools for clustering event catalogues in space–time [33, 37], separating background from clustered activity (declustering), and even using per-event location uncertainty [34]. These tools are starting to be tested on lab AE [35].

3.3 Tensions and risks to address in your work

  • HDBSCAN [4] already selects clusters by stability without ε. You need a clear argument for why an explicit, physically interpretable ε, plus the noise constraint, is preferable in AE monitoring. Examples: reproducibility across tests, and thresholds engineers can interpret.
  • Lab AE may not show a clean clustered/background split [35]. The "benign noise" interpretation must therefore be demonstrated, not assumed.
  • Uncertainty models can be wrong. If Σ is underestimated, a Mahalanobis-type distance inflates separations. If Σ is overestimated, it merges clusters. Poorly located events with very large Σ can act as bridges between clusters. Sensitivity to Σ mis-specification is a necessary experiment.
  • Stability is not the same as correctness [10]. A plateau can be stable yet wrong, so ground-truth experiments (synthetic data, crack maps) are essential.
