1. The research topic in plain words

When a material cracks, it releases a tiny burst of energy that travels through the material as an elastic (sound-like) wave, usually in the 20 kHz to 1 MHz range, well above human hearing. Sensors glued to the surface record these bursts. Each burst is an acoustic emission (AE) event. Using the arrival times at several sensors, you can estimate where each event came from, much as seismologists locate earthquakes.

A test or monitoring campaign can produce thousands to millions of located events. Plotted in 3-D, they look like a cloud of points. Some points form dense streaks or patches: an active crack that keeps emitting. Others are scattered on their own: friction at supports, rubbing crack faces, one-off micro-cracks, or electrical noise. The practical question is:

The core question Which events belong together as one active damage zone (a "damage cluster"), and which are isolated background activity?

Answering that is a clustering problem. DBSCAN (Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise) is a natural tool because it (a) does not need the number of cracks in advance, (b) finds clusters of any shape, such as an elongated crack path, and (c) explicitly labels isolated points as "noise". Applied naively, however, DBSCAN has two weaknesses that your topic targets:

  1. The radius ε is chosen ad hoc. DBSCAN needs a neighbourhood radius ε. Results change drastically with ε, and in most AE papers it is picked by eye.
  2. Plain Euclidean distance ignores localization uncertainty. An event located near the centre of the sensor array may be accurate to about a millimetre, while one located outside the array may be off by a centimetre or more, and mostly in one direction. Treating both locations as equally reliable points is physically wrong.

Your definition proposes UA-DBSCAN (uncertainty-aware multi-scale DBSCAN) with three ingredients:

IngredientWhat it saysPlain meaning
(i) DistanceCombine source location and event time, scaled by per-event localization uncertainty.Two events are "close" if they are within a few standard errors of each other in space, and close in time.
(ii) ε-selection rulePick ε from a plateau in the cluster-count curve, subject to a noise-fraction constraint.Try many ε values. Choose one where the number of clusters stays stable and the share of noise is reasonable.
(iii) Noise as a classTreat DBSCAN's noise label as "isolated, benign activity", not discarded data.Unclustered events are a meaningful third category to report and interpret.

The rest of this guide builds every concept you need to understand, implement and critique those three ideas.

2. Acoustic emission: the physics and the data

2.1 What produces acoustic emission

AE is the transient elastic wave generated by a rapid, local release of strain energy inside a material. Typical sources are crack initiation and growth, fibre breakage, matrix cracking and delamination in composites, dislocation movement in metals, friction between crack faces, and corrosion processes [14, 15]. AE is a passive method: the structure produces the signal itself, and you only listen. It detects active damage, meaning damage that is growing now, not damage that simply exists. That is why grouping events in space and time is so informative: a cluster that keeps growing is an active crack.

The same physics appears at very different scales. Lockner [16] showed that AE in rock specimens behaves like "laboratory earthquakes", and several ideas you will use come from earthquake seismology (Section 13, and Part F of the literature review).

2.2 Hits, events and waveform features

A hit is one sensor detecting one signal, usually when the voltage crosses a preset threshold (for example 40 dB). An event is the physical emission, recognised when several sensors register hits within a short time window consistent with wave travel across the array. Only events with enough hits, typically at least 4 in 3-D, can be located.

From each waveform, classical parameters are extracted: peak amplitude (in dBAE), counts (threshold crossings), duration, rise time (from first threshold crossing to peak), energy, and frequency descriptors. Two derived quantities are widely used in concrete:

  • RA value = rise time / amplitude (ms/V)
  • Average frequency (AF) = counts / duration (kHz)

Tensile cracking tends to give low RA and high AF, while shear-type cracking gives high RA and low AF. This is the basis of the RILEM recommendation [17] and of later studies [18, 19]. These features are not needed for spatial clustering, but they are very useful for validating what your clusters and noise class mean (Section 9).

2.3 Other quantities you will meet

  • b-value: the slope of the amplitude–frequency (Gutenberg–Richter-type) distribution of events. A drop in b-value is associated with a shift from distributed micro-cracking to localized macro-cracking [20].
  • Kaiser effect / Felicity ratio: a material that has already been loaded tends not to emit again until the previous maximum load is exceeded. Emission below the previous maximum signals damage.
  • Moment tensor analysis (e.g. SiGMA) uses first-motion amplitudes to classify the crack type and orientation of individual events [14].

2.4 Waves and why they matter for location

In a bulk solid, energy travels as P-waves (fastest, longitudinal) and S-waves (slower, transverse). In thin plates it travels as dispersive Lamb waves, whose speed depends on frequency. Location algorithms usually assume a single known wave speed v. Every departure from that assumption adds location error: heterogeneity (aggregate in concrete), anisotropy (composites), existing cracks that block or detour the path, and dispersion. This is the physical reason each located event has its own, different uncertainty.

3. Locating an AE source, and why every location is uncertain

3.1 Time-difference-of-arrival (TDOA) location

Let the unknown source position be x = (x, y, z) with unknown origin time t0. Sensor i sits at a known position si and records the arrival time ti. With a constant wave speed v:

ti = t0 + ‖x − si‖ / v + ei,   i = 1…N (3.1)

where ei is the timing error. There are 4 unknowns, (x, y, z, t0), so at least 4 sensors are needed in 3-D; more sensors give an over-determined least-squares problem. The classical solver is Geiger's method: linearize (3.1) around a guess, solve a least-squares update, and iterate. Kundu [23] reviews this and many alternatives, including methods that do not need a known wave speed. Grosse, Reinhardt and Dahm [21] describe the practical workflow in concrete.

3.2 Where the errors come from

  1. Onset (arrival-time) picking error. The first arrival is buried in noise, and a fixed threshold picks it late for weak signals. Automatic pickers such as the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) picker reduce this error [22]. Picking error is often the dominant term.
  2. Wave speed error. The assumed v is wrong, varies with direction, or changes as damage accumulates.
  3. Path effects. Cracks, holes or geometry make the true path longer than the straight line.
  4. Sensor geometry. This is the key one for your topic. The same timing error produces a small location error for a source inside a well-spread array, and a large, strongly elongated error for a source near or outside the array edge, or when sensors are almost collinear. Yin et al. [27] analyse this theoretically for sensor-cluster arrangements.

3.3 From timing error to a covariance matrix (the key derivation)

Collect the unknowns as θ = (x, t0). Linearizing (3.1) at the solution gives the Jacobian J, an N×4 matrix whose row i is

Ji = [ (x − si)T / (v ‖x − si‖) ,  1 ] (3.2)

The first three entries are the unit vector from sensor i to the source, divided by v. If the timing errors are independent with standard deviation σt, the standard least-squares result is

Cov(θ̂) ≈ σt2 (JTJ)−1,    Σk = upper-left 3×3 block for event k (3.3)

Σk is the per-event localization covariance. Geometrically it is an error ellipsoid: its axes point in the directions of largest and smallest uncertainty, and its semi-axis lengths are proportional to the square roots of the eigenvalues. Equation (3.3) makes the role of sensor geometry clear. When all the unit vectors from the sensors to the source point roughly the same way (source outside the array), the columns of J become nearly dependent, JTJ is nearly singular, and the ellipsoid becomes long and thin. In satellite navigation this effect is called "geometric dilution of precision" (GDOP).

3.4 Bayesian and data-driven localization

Equation (3.3) is a first-order (linearized) approximation. A more complete approach treats location as Bayesian inference: combine a likelihood from the arrival times, with uncertain wave speed and picking error treated as random variables, with a prior, and obtain a full posterior distribution of the source location. Schumacher, Straub and Higgins [25] introduced this for AE, and Madarshahian, Ziehl and Caicedo [26] extended it to handle onset-time uncertainty explicitly. Hensman et al. [24] used Gaussian processes to learn arrival-time maps in complex geometries, which also gives predictive uncertainty. Any of these can supply the per-event Σk that UA-DBSCAN needs. A Bayesian posterior can even supply a non-Gaussian location distribution.

Take-away for your research UA-DBSCAN assumes that every event comes with an uncertainty estimate. Decide early how you will get it: (a) linearized covariance (3.3), cheap and standard; (b) Bayesian posterior [25, 26], richer but costly; or (c) empirical, from pencil-lead-break (Hsu–Nielsen) tests at known positions. Whichever you choose becomes part of your method section.

4. Clustering: the basic idea and the main families

Clustering means grouping objects so that objects in the same group are more similar to each other than to objects in other groups, without any labels telling you the right answer. That makes it unsupervised learning. Everything depends on (1) how you measure similarity (the distance) and (2) what you mean by a "group" (the cluster model).

FamilyExampleCluster modelGood for AE locations?
Partitioningk-means, k-medoidsk compact, roughly spherical blobs around centresPoor: needs k in advance, dislikes elongated crack shapes, forces every event into a cluster

In the AE literature, most clustering work has been done in feature space (amplitude, frequency, RA and so on) to separate damage mechanisms, such as matrix cracking versus fibre breakage, usually with k-means or similar [28, 29, 32]. Your topic is different: it clusters in location–time space to identify damage zones. The two are complementary, and many strong papers combine them.

5. DBSCAN in detail

DBSCAN was introduced by Ester, Kriegel, Sander and Xu in 1996 [1]. It is one of the most cited data-mining algorithms. It has only two parameters:

  • ε (eps): the neighbourhood radius.
  • MinPts (called min_samples in scikit-learn): the minimum number of points, including the point itself, that must lie within ε for a region to count as dense.

5.1 Definitions

  • ε-neighbourhood of point p: Nε(p) = { q : dist(p, q) ≤ ε }.
  • Core point: |Nε(p)| ≥ MinPts.
  • Directly density-reachable: q is directly reachable from p if p is a core point and q ∈ Nε(p).
  • Density-reachable: there is a chain p → … → q of direct reachability steps. Every point in the chain except possibly the last must be a core point.
  • Density-connected: p and q are both density-reachable from some core point o.
  • Cluster: a maximal set of density-connected points.
  • Border point: a non-core point inside some core point's neighbourhood. It belongs to a cluster but does not extend it.
  • Noise: any point that is neither core nor border.

5.2 The algorithm

for each point p not yet visited:
    mark p visited
    N = RangeQuery(p, eps)            # all points within eps of p
    if |N| < MinPts: label p NOISE (may later become a border point)
    else:
        start new cluster C; add p to C
        seeds = N \ {p}
        for each q in seeds:
            if q was NOISE: add q to C (border point)
            if q not visited:
                mark q visited
                Nq = RangeQuery(q, eps)
                if |Nq| >= MinPts: seeds = seeds ∪ Nq   # q is core: expand
            if q not in any cluster: add q to C

Cost is dominated by the range queries. With a spatial index (k-d tree, R*-tree, cover tree) and a small ε, it is roughly O(n log n) in practice. The worst case is O(n2). Schubert et al. [5] discuss this in detail and point out that DBSCAN works with any distance function if you give up index acceleration. This matters because your UA distance is not Euclidean.

5.3 Properties worth memorising

  • Core points and noise points are assigned deterministically. Border points that are within ε of two clusters may be assigned to either, depending on processing order.
  • A single global ε means one global density threshold. DBSCAN therefore struggles when clusters have very different densities, such as a highly active main crack next to a slowly growing secondary crack. This is the motivation for OPTICS and HDBSCAN, and for your "multi-scale" idea.
  • If ε is too small, almost everything is noise. If ε is too large, everything merges into one cluster. Somewhere in between is a useful range.

5.4 Classical parameter heuristics

  • MinPts: Ester et al. [1] used MinPts = 4 for 2-D data. Sander et al. [2] suggest MinPts = 2 × dimensionality. For location plus time (4-D) that gives about 8. Larger values give smoother, more noise-robust results.
  • ε by k-distance plot: compute each point's distance to its k-th nearest neighbour (k = MinPts − 1), sort these distances in decreasing order, plot them, and look for a "knee" or "elbow". Points to the right of the knee are in dense regions. This is the heuristic of [1]. Automating the knee detection is a small literature of its own [8, 9].
  • Red flags (Schubert et al. [5], §4.2): a useful DBSCAN result should typically have a noise fraction between about 1% and 30%. If the largest cluster holds more than 20–50% of the clustered points, ε is probably too large, or a hierarchical method (OPTICS or HDBSCAN) is needed. They also plot the number of clusters against ε. This is the closest published precedent for the ε rule in your definition, so read it carefully.

6. Relatives of DBSCAN you must know

GDBSCAN (Sander et al., 1998) [2]

This generalizes DBSCAN: "within ε" can be replaced by any symmetric, reflexive neighbourhood predicate, and "count ≥ MinPts" by any weighted cardinality. That is the theoretical licence for UA-DBSCAN. You can define "neighbour" as "within ε in uncertainty-scaled space–time distance", and all of DBSCAN's cluster definitions still hold.

ST-DBSCAN (Birant & Kut, 2007) [6]

This uses two radii, ε1 for space and ε2 for time (or a non-spatial attribute). Two points are neighbours only if both conditions hold. It is the most direct prior art for combining location and time. UA-DBSCAN differs by (a) merging space and time into one scaled distance, and (b) scaling space by per-event uncertainty. You should compare against ST-DBSCAN as a baseline.

OPTICS (Ankerst et al., 1999) [3]

OPTICS does not produce one clustering. It produces an ordering of the points with a reachability distance for each. The resulting reachability plot shows valleys (clusters) at all ε values at once, like a multi-scale picture. A DBSCAN result for any ε can be read off by cutting the plot horizontally.

HDBSCAN (Campello, Moulavi & Sander, 2013) [4]

HDBSCAN builds the full hierarchy of DBSCAN-like clusterings over all density levels, then extracts the clusters that are most stable, meaning they persist over the widest range of density levels. It effectively removes ε and keeps only a minimum cluster size. Your "plateau in the cluster-count curve" is a related, simpler notion of stability. HDBSCAN is therefore both a strong baseline and a conceptual cousin, and you must explain what UA-DBSCAN adds (physically interpretable ε, uncertainty-aware distance, a noise constraint).

FDBSCAN, density-based clustering of uncertain data (Kriegel & Pfeifle, 2005) [7]

FDBSCAN treats each object as a probability distribution rather than a point, and defines a probabilistic ("fuzzy") distance: the probability that two uncertain objects are within ε of each other. It is the closest data-mining ancestor of your idea (i). UA-DBSCAN can be seen as a physically specified version, where the distributions come from sensor geometry and wave physics rather than being given abstractly.

Where UA-DBSCAN sits Plain DBSCAN [1] → a general neighbourhood predicate (GDBSCAN [2]) → adding time (ST-DBSCAN [6]) → adding uncertainty (FDBSCAN [7]; ACLUD in seismology [34]) → multi-density and stability (OPTICS [3], HDBSCAN [4]). UA-DBSCAN combines the time, uncertainty and stability ideas, with every parameter tied to AE physics.

7. Part (i): an uncertainty-scaled space–time distance

7.1 Why Euclidean distance is wrong here

Take two events 8 mm apart. If each has a location uncertainty of about 1 mm, they are almost certainly different sources. If each is uncertain by 10 mm along the line joining them, they could easily be the same source. Euclidean distance treats both cases identically. The statistically natural fix is the Mahalanobis distance, which measures separation in units of standard deviation along each direction.

7.2 One concrete form (a starting proposal)

Your definition does not fix the formula, so choosing and justifying it is part of your research. A natural first choice for events i and j with estimated locations x̂i, x̂j, covariances Σi, Σj and times ti, tj is:

dij2 = (x̂i − x̂j)T (Σi + Σj)−1 (x̂i − x̂j) + ( (ti − tj) / τ )2 (7.1)
  • Spatial term: if both events came from the same true point and their location errors are independent and Gaussian, then x̂i − x̂j ~ N(0, Σi + Σj), and the spatial term follows a χ²-distribution with 3 degrees of freedom (2 in 2-D).
  • Temporal term: τ is a time scale that says how far apart in time two events can be and still count as the same activity. It could be tied to the loading rate, a load step, or a characteristic event rate.
  • The result is dimensionless, so ε is a number of standard deviations rather than millimetres. This is what makes the ε-rule physics-informed.
A useful consequence (idea to develop) Because the spatial term is χ²3-distributed under the "same source" hypothesis, the 95% quantile χ²3, 0.95 ≈ 7.81 suggests εspace ≈ √7.81 ≈ 2.8 as a physically motivated reference value. You can then check whether the plateau rule of part (ii) lands near it. Agreement would be strong evidence for the method; disagreement would reveal model error, such as underestimated Σ.

7.3 Alternatives you should compare

  • Bhattacharyya or Hellinger distance between the two Gaussian location distributions. These include a term that penalises very different covariances.
  • Probability of proximity: P(‖Xi − Xj‖ ≤ r), where Xi and Xj are the random true positions of events i and j (their uncertain locations) and r is a physical radius. This is the FDBSCAN style [7].
  • Space–time nearest-neighbour proximity from seismology (Zaliapin & Ben-Zion [33]), which combines distance, time and magnitude. It has been applied to lab AE by Matochkina et al. [35].
  • Separate thresholds (ST-DBSCAN [6]) instead of one combined distance.

7.4 Important technical caveats

  • Formula (7.1) with pair-dependent Σi + Σj is not a true metric: the triangle inequality can fail. DBSCAN (via GDBSCAN) still works because it only needs symmetry, but tree indexes that assume a metric cannot be used blindly. A safe speed-up is a Euclidean pre-filter: if dij ≤ ε then ‖x̂i − x̂j‖ ≤ ε·√λmax(Σi + Σj). So first find Euclidean candidates within that radius, using the largest λmax in the dataset, then compute the exact distance only for those pairs.
  • The large-uncertainty trap. A very poorly located event has a huge Σ, so its Mahalanobis distance to everything is small. It then becomes a neighbour of many events and may even become a core point that bridges two separate cracks. You need a rule for this: reject events with det Σ or λmax above a threshold, assign them to a separate "unlocatable" class, or use a distance that penalises large covariance (Bhattacharyya). This is a genuine research question, and a place where your work can contribute.

8. Part (ii): choosing ε from a plateau in the cluster-count curve

8.1 The idea

Run DBSCAN for a sequence of ε values, usually on a logarithmic grid. For each, record the number of clusters K(ε) and the noise fraction η(ε), the share of events labelled noise.

  • η(ε) decreases (weakly) monotonically as ε grows: more neighbours means fewer isolated points.
  • K(ε) is not monotone. At tiny ε there are almost no clusters because everything is noise. K rises as small groups form, then falls as groups merge, and finally reaches 1 when everything is one cluster.
  • A plateau is a range of ε over which K does not change. A wide plateau means the partition is stable: the grouping does not depend on the exact ε you pick, which suggests it reflects real structure. This is the same intuition as clustering stability [10] and HDBSCAN's cluster stability [4].
  • The noise-fraction constraint ηmin ≤ η(ε) ≤ ηmax rules out degenerate plateaus, such as the trivial "everything is noise" or "one giant cluster" regimes. Schubert et al.'s 1–30% band [5] is a starting point. The physically right band for AE is something you can study.

8.2 A precise version you can implement

  1. Choose a grid ε1 < ε2 < … < εM (for example 60 values, log-spaced from 0.3 to 30 in UA units).
  2. For each εm, run DBSCAN and store the labels, Km and ηm.
  3. Keep only the indices with ηmin ≤ ηm ≤ ηmax.
  4. Find the runs of consecutive admissible indices with the same K. Measure each run's width as log(εend / εstart).
  5. Choose the widest run, and set ε* to the geometric mean of its endpoints.
  6. Report the second-widest plateau as well. If it is comparably wide, the data have structure at two scales (Section 10).
Weakness to fix: equal counts do not mean equal clusters K can stay constant while the actual membership changes (one cluster splits while two others merge). A stronger plateau criterion is that the Adjusted Rand Index [13] between partitions at neighbouring ε values stays close to 1. Consider proposing "partition-stability plateaus" rather than "count plateaus". This is a clean, defensible methodological improvement.

9. Part (iii): noise as an explicit "isolated, benign activity" class

Most DBSCAN applications throw noise points away. Your definition instead gives them meaning: events that are not density-connected to any damage cluster, given their uncertainty, represent isolated, benign activity. Examples are friction at loading points, sporadic micro-cracking that does not coalesce, and settling. Reporting this class matters for structural health monitoring. An operator wants to know that "200 events occurred, of which 170 are isolated and harmless", not just to see those 170 silently dropped.

9.1 How to justify "benign" (you must not simply assume it)

  • Waveform features: compare noise and cluster events on RA, AF, amplitude, energy and b-value [17–20]. If noise events are systematically lower in energy or amplitude and do not show the progressive b-value drop seen in clusters, that supports "benign".
  • Temporal behaviour: benign activity should be roughly stationary (Poisson-like) in time, while active cracks accelerate.
  • Ground truth: after a lab test, compare with the visible crack pattern, digital image correlation (DIC), or CT scans. Noise events should not lie on final crack paths.

9.2 Things that can land in the noise class but are not benign

  • The first few events of a new crack: before MinPts events have accumulated, a nascent crack looks isolated. A temporal (streaming) analysis can show events migrating from noise into a cluster. That is interesting in itself as an early-warning indicator.
  • Badly located events, if you exclude them via a Σ threshold (Section 7.4). Keep them in a separate "unlocatable" class, not in "benign".
  • Electrical or mechanical noise that passed the event-definition filters.

A careful paper would therefore present four labels: damage clusters, isolated benign, unlocatable (large Σ), and optionally transitional (noise events that later join a cluster).

10. What "physics-informed" and "multi-scale" mean here

Physics-informed here does not mean physics-informed neural networks (PINNs). It means that the clustering's ingredients are derived from the physics of the measurement rather than tuned by hand:

  • Σk comes from wave-propagation and arrival-time physics (Equation 3.3 or a Bayesian posterior).
  • ε has a statistical and physical meaning (a number of standard errors, with a χ² reference).
  • τ can be tied to the loading protocol or the damage-process time scale.
  • MinPts can be tied to the minimum number of events that engineers accept as evidence of an active source.

Multi-scale refers to damage organising at several scales. Diffuse micro-cracking forms broad, low-density zones, while a localized macro-crack forms a dense, narrow band. A single ε sees only one scale. Three ways to make the method multi-scale:

  1. Multiple plateaus: if the cluster-count curve has two wide plateaus, report both partitions as fine-scale and coarse-scale damage structure.
  2. Hierarchical relation: show that fine-scale clusters nest inside coarse-scale ones. This is essentially what OPTICS and HDBSCAN produce, so compare against them.
  3. Multi-scale in time: sweep τ as well as ε, to separate short bursts (crack jumps) from slow persistent activity.

11. How to evaluate UA-DBSCAN

11.1 Synthetic benchmark (essential, because it gives ground truth)

  1. Define a specimen geometry and a sensor layout.
  2. Place true damage sources, for example points along 2–3 crack lines or planes with different activity rates, and add spatially uniform "benign" background events.
  3. For each true source, compute exact arrival times using Equation (3.1), add picking noise (σt) and wave-speed error, and re-locate the event with your solver. This gives realistic x̂ and Σ, including the geometry effects in Figure 2.
  4. Cluster the result with plain DBSCAN (k-distance ε), ST-DBSCAN, OPTICS, HDBSCAN and UA-DBSCAN.
  5. Score against the truth (below). Repeat for different σt, sensor layouts and background levels. This sensitivity study is often the most convincing part of a methods paper.

11.2 Metrics

MetricNeeds ground truth?Use
Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) [13]YesAgreement between the found and true partitions, corrected for chance. 1 = perfect.
Noise precision and recallYesHow well the noise class captures the true benign events.
DBCV (Density-Based Clustering Validation) [11]NoInternal validity index designed for density-based, arbitrarily shaped clusters.

11.3 Real data

Typical experiments are three- or four-point bending of concrete or reinforced concrete beams, compact-tension specimens, composite coupons, or rock cores under compression [16, 21, 30]. Compare clusters with final crack maps, DIC strain fields or CT. Pencil-lead breaks (Hsu–Nielsen sources) at known positions give a controlled way to measure real localization error.

12. A practical starting implementation (Python sketch)

This is a minimal, readable sketch to get started. It is not optimized and not a finished method. It uses NumPy and scikit-learn's DBSCAN with a precomputed distance matrix, which is fine up to a few thousand events. For larger catalogues, switch to a sparse matrix built with the Euclidean pre-filter from Section 7.4.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.cluster import DBSCAN

def ua_distance_matrix(X, S, t, tau):
    """X: (n,3) locations, S: (n,3,3) covariances, t: (n,) times, tau: time scale."""
    n = len(X)
    D = np.zeros((n, n))
    for i in range(n):
        dx = X[i] - X[i+1:]                      # (m,3)
        C = S[i] + S[i+1:]                       # (m,3,3) pairwise summed covariance
        sol = np.linalg.solve(C, dx[..., None])[..., 0]
        d2 = np.einsum('mk,mk->m', dx, sol) + ((t[i] - t[i+1:]) / tau) ** 2
        D[i, i+1:] = D[i+1:, i] = np.sqrt(d2)
    return D

def epsilon_sweep(D, min_pts, eps_grid):
    out = []
    for eps in eps_grid:
        lab = DBSCAN(eps=eps, min_samples=min_pts, metric="precomputed").fit_predict(D)
        K = len(set(lab)) - (1 if -1 in lab else 0)
        eta = np.mean(lab == -1)
        out.append((eps, K, eta, lab))
    return out

def pick_plateau(sweep, eta_min=0.01, eta_max=0.30):
    best, run = None, []
    for eps, K, eta, lab in sweep + [(None, None, None, None)]:
        ok = eta is not None and eta_min <= eta <= eta_max and K > 0
        if ok and (not run or run[-1][1] == K):
            run.append((eps, K)); continue
        if run:
            width = np.log(run[-1][0] / run[0][0])
            if best is None or width > best[0]:
                best = (width, run[0][0], run[-1][0], run[0][1])
        run = [(eps, K)] if ok else []
    if best is None: return None
    width, e0, e1, K = best
    return dict(eps=np.sqrt(e0 * e1), K=K, plateau=(e0, e1), log_width=width)

13. Open questions and pitfalls: where your contribution can be

These are proposals for you to evaluate, not established results. Check the literature again before claiming novelty, since the field moves quickly.

QuestionWhy it mattersHow to test
Does the plateau-selected ε agree with the χ² reference (≈2.8 in 3-D)?Agreement would show that ε is physically meaningful rather than empirical.Synthetic data with known Σ; then real data with Σ from pencil-lead calibration.
Is uncertainty scaling better than Euclidean distance when sensor geometry is poor?This is the central claim of part (i).ARI versus distance-to-array-centroid, for plain DBSCAN versus UA-DBSCAN.
How should large-Σ events be handled?Mahalanobis scaling makes them "close to everything" (Section 7.4).Compare gating, Bhattacharyya distance, and an "unlocatable" class.
Count plateau or partition-stability plateau?Equal counts can hide different partitions.ARI between neighbouring ε values; compare selection accuracy.
How should τ be chosen?Time scaling is as ad hoc as ε unless it is tied to physics.Tie τ to loading rate or event rate; sweep jointly with ε.
Is the noise class really benign?This is the central claim of part (iii).Feature statistics (RA/AF, energy, b-value) and ground-truth crack maps.
How does UA-DBSCAN compare with HDBSCAN, and with ACLUD from seismology [34]?Reviewers will ask. ACLUD already uses location uncertainty to cluster earthquakes.Include both as baselines, or explain clearly why ACLUD's goal (fitting fault planes) differs.
Can it run online?Monitoring needs incremental updates, and the moment an event joins a cluster is an early-warning signal.A streaming DBSCAN variant; measure detection latency.

14. A suggested first-semester roadmap

  1. Weeks 1–2: read the core papers in this order: [1], [5], [6], [4], [7], [23], [25], [15], [34]. Use the companion literature review for summaries.
  2. Weeks 3–4: build the simulator. Write the arrival-time forward model (3.1), a Geiger-style locator, and the covariance (3.3). Reproduce the pattern in Figure 2 with real numbers for your sensor layout.
  3. Weeks 5–6: baselines. Run plain DBSCAN (k-distance ε), ST-DBSCAN and HDBSCAN on synthetic catalogues, with ARI and noise precision/recall.
  4. Weeks 7–8: UA-DBSCAN version 1. Implement distance (7.1) and the plateau rule, then compare.
  5. Weeks 9–10: robustness. Vary σt, sensor layout, background rate and cluster density ratio; handle large-Σ events.
  6. Weeks 11–12: real data. Obtain a lab AE dataset (your own test, a collaborator's, or a published one) with a known crack pattern, and validate the noise class with waveform features.
  7. Weeks 13–14: write-up. Draft the methods section and figures. Discuss the target venue with your supervisor; typical options are Mechanical Systems and Signal Processing, Ultrasonics, NDT & E International, Structural Health Monitoring, and the Journal of Nondestructive Evaluation.

15. Glossary

TermMeaning
Acoustic emission (AE)Transient elastic waves generated by rapid energy release in a material, and the monitoring method based on detecting them.
Hit / EventHit: one sensor's detection. Event: one physical source, seen as hits on several sensors.
ThresholdVoltage level (in dB) a signal must exceed to be recorded.
Onset pickingEstimating the arrival time of the first wave; AIC is a common automatic method.
TDOATime difference of arrival, the basis of source location.
Geiger's methodIterative linearized least-squares solver for source location.
Covariance matrix ΣDescribes the size, shape and orientation of the location uncertainty (error ellipsoid).
Mahalanobis distanceDistance measured in standard-deviation units using a covariance matrix.
χ² distributionDistribution of a sum of squared standard normal variables; used to set statistically meaningful thresholds.
DBSCANDensity-based clustering with a radius ε and a minimum count MinPts; labels core, border and noise points.
k-distance plotSorted distances to the k-th nearest neighbour; the "knee" suggests ε.
OPTICS / HDBSCANDensity-based methods that consider all ε values at once (ordering / hierarchy).
ST-DBSCANDBSCAN with separate spatial and temporal radii.
PlateauA range of ε over which the clustering result (cluster count) does not change.
Noise fraction ηShare of events that DBSCAN labels as noise.
ARIAdjusted Rand Index; chance-corrected agreement between two partitions.
DBCVDensity-Based Clustering Validation; an internal quality index for density-based clusters.
RA / AFRise time / amplitude, and counts / duration; used to classify tensile versus shear cracking.
b-valueSlope of the amplitude distribution; it drops as damage localizes.
Kaiser effectAbsence of AE on reloading below the previous maximum load.
Hsu–Nielsen sourcePencil-lead break used as a repeatable artificial AE source for calibration.
